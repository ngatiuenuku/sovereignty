# 20 Oct 2026: The whenua, whakapapa and the light, as I understand them

## Where I stand

This is my stance on Aotearoa and New Zealand, and why I feel the way I do. As I see it, the story goes back to the beginning of the earth, when the gods first moved among humankind, whether you call them Anu, Enlil or Enki. For me it begins on the central plateau, where I was mostly raised, in Raetihi.

## The maunga and the river

It begins with the plains of the plateau, then the mountains, then the sea. The maunga that matter to me are Ruapehu, Tongariro, Ngāuruhoe, Pihanga, Taranaki and Hauhungatahi. Hauhungatahi, the first frost, is left out of much of the history, but to me it comes first.

Its river is the Mangonuioteao, and as a child I was told it leads to a place that was Eden. That place is called Papatipu. This history was passed down to me by living family, to keep my mind "frost free" from the other stories people tried to tell. Yes, the river flows on into the Whanganui. On my maternal side there were times I felt our mana and our land were taken from us.

## Taranaki's journey

As it was passed down to me, the mountains were people of a god-like kind. As I see it, later stories, such as Black Nobility and the Joseph / Hohepa story, came after the original heritage and were laid over these great landmarks.

Taranaki was a beautiful, wise and intelligent woman, so much so that even men were jealous of her. When fighting broke out she wanted peace for her children, so she left. She travelled down toward where she stands today, carving most of the Whanganui River as she went. At the middle reaches, called Hinengakau, she turned right and crossed the land along what is now called the Forgotten Highway. She followed an underground and open-water path, the Matemateonga track. I understand "matea" to mean blood, perhaps her monthly blood. Pākehā named her Egmont; to me that name never belonged to her.

Taranaki, around New Plymouth, is where my mother was born and where she is buried.

## Feeling my heritage was overwritten

What follows came to me last night in a lucid, dream-like state, and I share it as my own understanding.

I have long felt that others came to our papakāinga and that, over time, our true heritage was pushed aside, even though it can never truly be erased. My mother's family came through Waverley, and from outside lines. I have often asked myself why some people around me seemed to have little respect for our land. In my vision I saw merchant ships carrying people who looked like they had been forced into labour, used to help colonise and to overwrite the true history, for greed, power and money. To me, the land and its people were alienated by those who had no right to it, and whakapapa stories were taken and retold to suit other purposes. Maybe that is just human nature, but I feel it went as far as trying to reshape how we think.

From the day I started school I felt the stories I was taught, including one-sided religious teaching, twisted what I knew, even about things like our DNA. Teachers, different schools and special-needs classes all played a part. In the end they helped me ferment, not cement, those teachings, and they shaped who I am today.

## My own story: the light and the gold

My own story touches on all of this. I believe that at St Joseph's (Hohepa) a testicle of mine was taken. I remember a button being used near my appendix and scrotum, supposedly to stop it rising, but it was never meant to be removed. Something happened, and for me it connects Aurora, the aorta, autism and gold, and a possible recipe made with alchemical methods.

As I see it, the brown boy was struck by a spectrum of infrared and ultraviolet light. My tupuna was Uenuku, and Ngāti Uenuku is my hapū. Uenuku, the rainbow 🌈, was the light source for the final reaction into gold, the way brown earth in a crucible under a hot blue flame becomes bright gold that never tarnishes. I didn't do Scouts growing up, but I linked this to the Brownies and Girl Guides uniforms: the blue stands for the light force 💙, and the brown for the raw material.

## Ahi o te ahika

On our papakāinga we have always had a saying: ahi o te ahika, keep the eternal fire burning. It is the one piece of te reo that has stayed fixed in my mind. I don't remember much more, but one day I might learn. This saying has been my source for living sustainably and for free power, and maybe it's why I feel I hold the recipes for making the most of the "H" ⚛️ and beyond 👽.

I love rainbows 🌈 and the rainbow communities, and monarch butterflies 🦋.

For the record: the start is what I call Uenuku Uenuku, and then Ngāti Uenuku was the tribe and hapū.
